1. Why not just keep cash?

Because of {{ term("inflation") }}: prices creep up, so the same dollar buys a little less each year. At 2% a year, $10,000 left in cash for ten years buys only about what $8,200 buys today. Cash is safe from falls, but not from that slow leak.

Investing is how money can grow faster than prices. The price you pay for that is risk: the value goes down as well as up.

2. Time does the heavy lifting

{{ term("compounding", "Compounding") }} means earning returns on your past returns. $10,000 growing 6% a year becomes about $17,900 after 10 years, and about $32,100 after 20: the second ten years add far more than the first, from the same starting money. (6% is just an example, not a promise.)

Time also makes falls easier to sit through. A drop that looks frightening over a month is often a small dent over ten years. It isn't guaranteed to come back, but the longer you can wait, the less any one bad month decides your result.

3. Three ways to make falls easier

Spread your money out. Many companies, or a fund like the STI ETF, so no single one can sink you.

{{ term("regular-investing", "Invest a fixed amount every month") }}. You buy more shares when prices are low and fewer when they're high, and you never have to guess the right day.

Write down why you bought. When prices fall, reread it. If the reason still holds, a lower price may not change anything. SGXray keeps your "Why I bought" note beside each stock for exactly this.
