1. A share: a piece of a business

Buy a {{ term("share") }} of DBS and you own a tiny part of the bank. When it makes a profit, part of that is paid to you as a {{ term("dividend") }}. Its price goes up and down with how the bank and the market are doing.

On SGX, shares usually trade in {{ term("board-lot", "board lots") }} of 100. You can buy fewer, from a single share, in SGX's unit share market.

2. A REIT: a piece of buildings

A {{ term("reit", "REIT") }} owns property, like malls, offices or warehouses, and collects the rent. Under Singapore's tax rules a REIT avoids tax at the trust level only if it pays out at least 90% of its taxable income, so REITs tend to pay bigger, steadier payouts than most companies.

The catch: paying almost everything out leaves little to grow with, so REITs often borrow or ask unitholders for more money to buy new buildings.

3. An ETF: a basket of many

An {{ term("etf", "ETF") }} is one fund holding many companies, bought like a single share. The STI ETF holds all 30 companies in the Straits Times Index, Singapore's 30 biggest listed companies, in one buy. If one company has a terrible year, the other 29 soften the blow.

Buying a stock because "everyone knows the name" without knowing what the business does or how it makes money. A familiar brand isn't the same as a good investment, and if you can't say in one sentence what you own, you won't know whether to hold on when its price falls.

{% endblock %} {% block question %}You own one STI ETF unit and DBS has a bad year. Does it affect you?{% endblock %} {% block answer %}

Yes, but only a little. DBS is one of the 30 companies inside the STI, and one of the biggest, so it's a larger slice of the ETF than most. But the other 29 companies make up the rest, so a bad year for DBS hurts far less than owning DBS shares alone.
