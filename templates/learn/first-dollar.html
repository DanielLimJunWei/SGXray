1. A cushion first

Before any investing, keep an {{ term("emergency-fund", "emergency fund") }}: cash for surprises like losing your job, a hospital bill or a broken laptop. MoneySense, the government's financial education site, suggests 3 to 6 months of your expenses, or 12 months if your income goes up and down.

Keep it somewhere safe and quick to reach, like a savings account or Singapore Savings Bonds. Not in shares, and not in your CPF, which you can't take out for emergencies.

2. Clear expensive debt

An unpaid credit card bill at a big Singapore bank costs about 28% a year in interest (POSB's rate is 27.8%). No sensible investment reliably earns that. Paying off {{ term("expensive-debt", "expensive debt") }} first is a guaranteed win; investing while carrying it is running up a down escalator.

3. Only money you can leave alone

Share prices go down as well as up, sometimes a lot, in a few days. Over many years the ups have usually won, but nobody knows what the next few months bring.

So invest only money you won't need for at least five years. Then a fall is something you can wait out, not something that forces you to sell at the bottom. School fees next year, a wedding, a flat deposit: that money belongs in savings, not shares.

Putting your emergency fund into shares "because it's just sitting there". Emergencies tend to arrive when markets are down too: a job loss in a recession is the classic case. Then you're forced to sell low, at exactly the wrong time.

{% endblock %} {% block question %}You have $8,000 saved, spend $1,500 a month, and owe $1,200 on a credit card. What comes first?{% endblock %} {% block answer %}

Pay off the $1,200 card. That stops a cost of about 28% a year. The $6,800 left covers about 4½ months of expenses: a good emergency fund. Only money saved on top of that is ready to invest.
